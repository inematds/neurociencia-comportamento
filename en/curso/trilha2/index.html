2.1

Conformity: Why We Follow the Group

Solomon Asch and the Psychology of Conformity

In 1951, Solomon Asch devised one of psychology's most elegant experiments. Participants judged which of 3 lines was the same length as a standard line. The answer was visually obvious. But there was a trick: all the other "participants" were actors instructed to give the wrong answer unanimously. Result: ~75% of participants agreed with the clearly wrong answer in at least one round. Only 25% never gave in.

In uncertain situations, we use the group’s behavior as an indicator of reality. “If everyone says it’s line C, maybe I’m wrong.” That’s the informational mechanism. The more ambiguous the situation, the stronger the influence: we accept the group as a source of information because, evolutionarily, the group was usually right.

Even knowing the group is wrong, we disagree out of fear. Normative influence is driven by the desire to belong. Publicly disagreeing leads to social exclusion, and the brain treats that exclusion as physical pain (the same neural network in the anterior cingulate cortex). We would rather be wrong together than right alone.

fMRI studies show that disagreeing with the group activates the amygdala (the fear and threat circuit). Agreeing with the group activates the nucleus accumbens (the reward circuit). Conformity isn’t just a social strategy: it’s neurologically pleasurable. Dissent is physiologically painful.

Asch demonstrated several key variations: (1) An ally dramatically reduces conformity. (2) Group size matters, but the effect levels off at ~4 members. (3) Unanimity is more powerful than size. (4) Responding privately (in writing) reduces conformity. (5) The group’s perceived status amplifies the effect. (6) Collectivist cultures show higher rates.

Digital technology amplified Asch: likes and shares are the new signals of conformity. The number of likes works like unanimous group approval. Viral content isn’t necessarily true; it’s socially approved. Algorithms create artificial unanimity by showing only popular content. The same Asch mechanism operates on a scale of billions.

2.2

Obedience to Authority: Milgram

The power of authority over moral conscience

In 1963, Stanley Milgram created psychology's most disturbing experiment. Participants were instructed by a researcher (an authority figure) to administer increasingly powerful electric shocks to an actor who simulated increasing pain. 65% administered the maximum 450-volt shock, potentially lethal, because the authority figure instructed them to. Most expressed discomfort, but obeyed.

Milgram discovered key factors: distance from the victim increases obedience (not seeing or hearing makes it easier). Perceived legitimacy (white lab coat, university laboratory, professional tone) suppresses moral resistance. When the experiment was conducted in a commercial office (without the Yale brand), obedience fell from 65% to 48%. The trappings of authority matter as much as authority itself.

When two actors refused to continue, only 10% of participants went all the way (compared with 65%). The presence of resisters dramatically reduces obedience. A single dissenter is enough to break the spell. This connects to Asch: unanimity, not numbers, is the deciding factor. Moral courage is contagious, just like submission.

Milgram coined the concept of the "agentic state": a person sees themselves as an instrument of authority, not as an autonomous moral agent. "I was just following orders." This mechanism shifts perceived responsibility to the authority. It is the same principle used in military chains of command and corporate hierarchies to diffuse blame.

Digital scams exploit perceived authority (emails from banks, the government, “technical support”). Organizational hierarchies elicit unethical behavior from employees who “are just following policy.” Crises increase submission: in moments of uncertainty, we delegate decisions to authority figures with even less questioning. Milgram predicted that modernity would increase, not reduce, obedience.

Milgram was inspired by Eichmann's trial and Hannah Arendt's concept of the "banality of evil": atrocities committed by ordinary people obeying orders. The experiment showed that you don't have to be a monster to do monstrous things. All it takes is a legitimate authority figure, emotional distance from the victim, and small, gradual increases in obedience.

2.3

Social Manipulation and Dark Patterns

Cialdini, persuasion, and manipulative design

Robert Cialdini (1984) identified 6 principles: (1) Reciprocity: the urge to return favors. (2) Commitment/Consistency: after taking a public position, we stick to it. (3) Social Proof: correct = what others do. (4) Authority: we follow experts. (5) Scarcity: rare = valuable. (6) Liking: we are persuaded by people we like. Each principle exploits a different cognitive shortcut.

Social proof: online reviews, follower counts, “best seller,” “trending.” We see others buying and we buy. Reciprocity: free samples create an obligation to give back. Supermarkets, restaurants, and sellers use this systematically. Together, these two principles explain much of modern commerce and marketing.

"Last units," "offer expires in 3h," "only 2 rooms left." Real or fabricated scarcity activates the loss aversion mechanism: losses weigh ~2x more than equivalent gains (Kahneman and Tversky). FOMO (fear of missing out) is social scarcity: not participating = missing out on something others have. Countdown + scarcity = impulse buying.

Dark patterns are interface designs that exploit cognitive biases: Roach Motel (easy to get in, hard to get out), Confirmshaming ("No, I prefer to pay more"), Privacy Zuckering (hidden settings), Fake countdown timers (artificial urgency), Scarcity illusion ("Only 2 left!"). Underlying mechanisms: loss aversion, anchoring, choice overload, and status quo inertia.

Each dark pattern exploits a specific bias. Anchoring: a price like "from R$199 to R$49" creates a false reference point. Default bias: preselected options capture data. Framing effect: "95% approval" vs. "5% disapprove." Decision fatigue: too many steps make people accept the default. Interface design is, essentially, choice architecture applied to the pixel.

GDPR (Europe) and LGPD (Brazil) are beginning to combat dark patterns in data consent, but enforcement is still limited. Individual defenses: recognize Cialdini's 6 principles when they are applied, slow down decisions (activate System 2), question artificial urgency, and verify sources of authority. Literacy in manipulation is the main defense against invisible persuasion.
