Obedience to Authority: The Milgram Experiment

In 1963, Stanley Milgram showed that ordinary people obey authority figures even when it means causing extreme suffering to another human being. 65% of participants went all the way to the maximum shock of 450 volts.

Stanley Milgram, a social psychologist at Yale, was intrigued by a question that haunted the post-Holocaust world: how did ordinary people manage to take part in atrocities? His answer was an experiment that would become one of psychology’s most famous and controversial.

The design was apparently simple. The participant (the "teacher") was instructed by a researcher in a white lab coat to administer increasingly strong electric shocks to another "participant" (actually an actor) each time he got a memory question wrong. The shocks ranged from 15 to 450 volts, in 15-volt increments. The labels on the panel ranged from "Mild Shock" to "XXX" (along with "Danger: Severe Shock").

DIAGRAM: Milgram's Obedience Chain

The actor never received real shocks. But his reactions were carefully staged: at 75 volts, he groaned; at 150, he asked to stop; at 300, he banged on the wall and refused to respond; at 330 volts and above, he fell completely silent. Even in the face of that silence, when the researcher said "please continue, the experiment requires you to continue," 65% of participants went up to 450 volts.

i

The prediction shock

Before the experiment, Milgram asked 40 psychiatrists what they expected. They predicted that less than 1% would go up to 450 volts. The actual rate was 65%. Obedience far exceeds what anyone, including experts, predicts.

Agentic state

Person sees themselves as someone else’s instrument and transfers responsibility

CONCEPT

Gradual increase

Each step is just 15V more, making escalation easier

CONCEPT

Destructive obedience

Following orders that harm others

Milgram conducted 19 variations of the original experiment to understand the factors that increase or reduce obedience. The results revealed that obedience is not a fixed personality trait, but rather a behavior shaped by situational variables.

VARIATIONS AND OBEDIENCE RATES

REMOTE PROXIMITY

Victim in another room, no sound

Obedience: 65%. Without sensory feedback, the victim is abstract. Psychological distance suppresses empathy.

VOCAL PROXIMITY

Victim heard (screams, pleas)

Obedience: 62.5%. The screams were disturbing, but most still continued. The authority figure’s presence was stronger than the distant screams.

PHYSICAL PROXIMITY

Victim in the same room

Obedience: 40%. Seeing the pain reduced obedience, but 4 in 10 still went all the way.

DIRECT TOUCH

Participant forced the victim's hand onto the plate

Obedience: 30%. Direct physical contact reduces obedience the most. Even so, nearly 1 in 3 obeyed.

ABSENT AUTHORITY

Researcher gave instructions over the phone

Obedience: 21%. The authority figure’s physical presence is crucial. Over the phone, many participants lied and said they were continuing.

The pattern is clear: the closer the victim is (and the farther away the authority is), the lower the obedience. But the perceived legitimacy of the authority is just as powerful. When the experiment was conducted at Yale (vs. an anonymous office), obedience was higher. The white coat, the professional tone, the institutional setting: all of this creates a perception of legitimate authority that suppresses moral resistance.

FACTORS THAT MODULATE OBEDIENCE

Increase obedience

✓ Victim distance
✓ Physical presence of authority
✓ Institutional legitimacy (Yale, lab coat)
✓ Gradual increase (15V at a time)

Reduce obedience

✗ Physical proximity to the victim
✗ Authority giving orders from a distance
✗ Noninstitutional setting
✗ Presence of dissenters

CONCEPT

Psychological distance

The less “real” the victim seems, the easier it is to obey

CONCEPT

Perceived legitimacy

Authority symbols (lab coat, institution) suppress resistance

CONCEPT

Moral disengagement

Mechanisms that allow harm to be caused without feeling guilt

The most hopeful variation of Milgram’s experiment involved two actors who served as “teachers” alongside the real participant. All three were supposed to administer shocks together. At one point, the first actor refused to continue. Then the second also refused. The real participant was left alone with the researcher.

The result is extraordinary: when two “teachers” refused before the participant did, obedience plummeted from 65% to just 10%. The presence of people who resist completely changes the social calculus. Collective resistance is not merely additive: it transforms the dynamic.

WITH RESISTORS vs. WITHOUT RESISTORS

No resisters (baseline)

✗Participant alone with an authority figure
✗No model of disobedience
✗65% go up to 450V
✗Disobeying = being "the different one"

With 2 dissenters

✓Two models of disobedience
✓Social "permission" to refuse
✓Only 10% go up to 450V
✓Disobeying = following the majority

This echoes Asch's variation with the ally: a single dissenter who breaks unanimity frees others to resist. Obedience and conformity share the same vulnerability: they depend on unanimity. Break the unanimity, and the system collapses.

!

The lesson for organizations

Anonymous reporting channels, protection for whistleblowers, and cultures that celebrate (instead of punish) constructive dissent work because they create "resisters" within the system. An organization that silences dissenters removes the natural protection against destructive obedience.

CONCEPT

Modeled resistance

CONCEPT

Breaking unanimity

CONCEPT

From 65% to 10%

Two dissenters reduce obedience by ~85%

The most effective digital scams do not exploit technical flaws. They exploit the psychology of obedience. The pattern is consistent: an authority figure (a bank, government, Receita Federal, or police) issues an urgent instruction that requires immediate action. The victim obeys before thinking critically, just like Milgram’s participants.

MILGRAM IN THE LAB vs. MILGRAM ON THE PHONE

Milgram (1963)

•Researcher in a white lab coat
•Laboratory setting at Yale
•"Continue, the experiment requires it"
•Gradual in-person pressure

Digital scam (2020s)

•Bank or Federal Revenue Service logo
•Interface identical to the official website
•"Your account will be blocked in 24h"
•Artificial urgency + malicious link

The elements are the same: perceived legitimacy (logos, formal language, domains that look official), urgency (a short deadline, a threat of consequences), and a specific request for action (click, transfer money, provide data). Urgency is especially effective because it activates System 1, preventing System 2 from conducting critical analysis.

!

Defense: the 5-minute rule

Milgram showed that urgency is authority's weapon. Any message demanding immediate action deserves exactly the opposite: a pause. Wait 5 minutes. Verify through another channel (call the bank, visit the official website by typing the URL). If the threat is real, 5 minutes won't change anything. If it's a scam, those 5 minutes can save everything.

CONCEPT

Social engineering

Manipulate people through authority and urgency, not code

CONCEPT

Urgency as a weapon

Short deadlines prevent critical analysis by System 2

CONCEPT

Authority spoofing

Imitate signs of legitimacy (logos, domains, language)

The Milgram experiment is more than a chapter in the history of psychology. It precisely describes how organizational hierarchies produce unethical decisions in everyday life. Enron, Volkswagen (Dieselgate), Boeing (737 MAX): in every case, ordinary people carried out instructions they knew were wrong because the order came "from above".

Corporate structures amplify the same mechanisms as Milgram’s experiments. The chain of command diffuses responsibility (each link says, "I was just doing what I was told"). Specialization fragments the view (each person sees only their piece, not the total harm). And in crises, urgency suppresses dissent: "we don’t have time to question this; we need to act."

MILGRAM IN ORGANIZATIONS: REAL CASES

2001

Enron

Employees manipulated the accounting at senior management's direction. Many knew it was fraud. No one challenged the authority of Kenneth Lay and Jeffrey Skilling.

2015

Volkswagen (Dieselgate)

Engineers implemented software to cheat emissions tests. The order came from the top. Engineers obeyed even though they knew it was illegal.

2018-2019

Boeing 737 MAX

Engineers reported problems with MCAS. Management prioritized the schedule over safety. 346 deaths in two crashes. The hierarchy silenced warnings.

KEY MECHANISM

Crises increase submission because they activate survival mode (System 1): the brain seeks a leader, reduces critical thinking, and prioritizes quick action over analysis. Milgram demonstrated this in the lab; corporate history demonstrates it in the real world.

CONCEPT

Moral fragmentation

CONCEPT

Crisis obedience

Urgency suppresses dissent and amplifies submission

The Milgram experiment sparked one of the greatest ethical controversies in the history of psychological research. In 1964, Diana Baumrind published a devastating critique: participants experienced extreme stress, some trembled uncontrollably, and they had not given informed consent for the actual experiment (they believed they were administering real shocks). Milgram deceived them, and the debriefing afterward did not necessarily erase the trauma of discovering they were capable of obeying to that extent.

ETHICAL ARGUMENTS

In favor of the experiment

✓ Revealed a fundamental truth about human behavior
✓ 84% of participants were "glad" to have taken part
✓ Impossible to obtain results any other way
✓ Transformed ethics into a field of research forever

Against the experiment

✗ Participants did not give informed consent
✗ Extreme stress: trembling, crying, seizures
✗ Potentially lasting psychological damage
✗ Deliberate deception violates participant autonomy

Ironically, Milgram's experiment directly contributed to the creation of research ethics committees (IRBs in the U.S., CEPs in Brazil). Today, a study like Milgram's would never be approved. But the paradox remains: we discovered the depth of human obedience only because the study was conducted in a way we now consider unethical.

i

Milgram's central lesson

Evil doesn't require evil people. It requires ordinary people in systems that distribute responsibility, legitimize authority, and isolate the victim. Destructive obedience is not about who we are, but about the system we're part of. Knowing this is the first line of defense.

While covering Adolf Eichmann’s trial in Jerusalem, Hannah Arendt coined the term “banality of evil”: the idea that major atrocities don’t require monsters, only obedient bureaucrats. Milgram provided empirical support for this idea. Destructive obedience is not a personality trait. It is a situational response to which we are all vulnerable.

Module 2.2 Summary

Milgram demonstrated that 65% of ordinary people obey an authority figure to the point of causing extreme harm to another human being. Obedience is shaped by the victim's distance, the authority figure's physical presence, and perceived legitimacy (lab coat, institution). Collective resistance is the most powerful defense: when two others refuse, obedience drops from 65% to 10%. Digital scams and corporate hierarchies exploit exactly the same mechanisms. The central lesson: evil doesn't require monsters; it requires systems that distribute responsibility and legitimize authority. Knowing this is the first line of defense.
