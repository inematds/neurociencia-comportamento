Do You Decide, or Does Your Brain Decide First?

Benjamin Libet (1983) · Readiness potential · Free will as veto

6 topics ~30 min Advanced
1

The Libet Experiment (1983)

Benjamin Libet, a neurophysiologist at the University of California, San Francisco, conducted one of the most disturbing experiments in the history of neuroscience in 1983. The protocol was simple: participants sat in front of an oscilloscope with a rotating pointer (like a clock) and were asked to flex their wrist whenever they wanted, spontaneously. The only additional task was to mentally note the pointer's position at the exact moment they felt the "urge" or "intention" to move.

While participants did this, EEG electrodes monitored the motor cortex’s electrical activity. The result was devastating to common-sense intuition: an electrical wave called the Readiness Potential (Bereitschaftspotential) began 350 to 500 milliseconds BEFORE participants reported being conscious of the intention to move. The brain had already “decided” before the person knew they were deciding.

Historical context

Libet was already known for his work on the timing of consciousness since the 1960s. His studies on direct cortical stimulation showed that ~500ms of continuous neural activity is needed for a stimulus to become conscious. The 1983 experiment applied this same logic to voluntary decision-making.

What the result means

The most direct interpretation of Libet's result is unsettling: the neural process that initiates a "voluntary" motor action precedes the conscious experience of intending to act. In other words, when you feel that you're "deciding" to move your finger, your brain has already begun preparing that movement hundreds of milliseconds earlier. Awareness of the decision comes after the neural event, not before.

This raises the hypothesis of epiphenomenalism: consciousness may be a byproduct of brain processes, not their cause. Like smoke rising from a locomotive: it accompanies the motion but doesn't produce it. If the brain decides before we know it, what role does consciousness play? Are we spectators to the decisions of our own nervous system?

What the result shows

  • ✓Neural activity precedes conscious awareness of intention
  • ✓The brain "prepares" actions before conscious will
  • ✓The timeline reverses the intuitive order (I want → I act)
  • ✓Unconscious processes take part in "voluntary" decisions

  • ✗That free will does not exist
  • ✗That consciousness is completely irrelevant
  • ✗That all human decisions are automatic
  • ✗That complex decisions follow the same pattern

The veto as free will

Libet himself, contrary to what many assume, did not conclude that free will does not exist. He observed that although the readiness potential precedes conscious awareness of the intention, participants could “veto” the movement in the last ~200ms before the action. In other words, consciousness does not initiate the decision, but it can cancel it. Libet proposed that genuine free will lies in this power to veto: “free won’t” instead of “free will.”

This reframing has profound implications. If the role of consciousness is not to initiate actions but to filter and censor unconscious impulses, then our sense of control over behavior needs to be reconsidered. We are not the original authors of our impulses, but we can be their editors. Conscious will would function like a gatekeeper that allows or blocks proposals generated unconsciously.

Current criticism and debates

In the decades following Libet’s experiment, a series of methodological and conceptual criticisms were raised. The main technical issue concerns the imprecision of the timing method: asking someone to report the exact moment of a subjective experience while looking at a clock introduces significant errors. The participant is estimating retroactively when the urge arose, not marking it in real time.

Aaron Schurger and colleagues (2012) proposed a radical reinterpretation: the readiness potential may not be preparation for a decision, but stochastic neural noise. When the noise level crosses a threshold by chance, the participant interprets it as “intention.” John-Dylan Haynes (2008) used fMRI and decoded intentions up to 10 seconds before conscious awareness, but with limited accuracy (~60%), only slightly above chance. After more than 40 years, there is no scientific consensus.

Schurger et al. (2012)

They proposed that the readiness potential reflects random fluctuations in cortical activity, not a preconscious decision. When the participant decides "now," they are simply waiting for these fluctuations to cross a threshold. If correct, Libet's experiment says nothing about free will.

Implications for the law

The modern legal system is built on the assumption that human beings are conscious agents capable of choosing their actions. Criminal liability requires two elements: actus reus (the prohibited act) and mens rea (the conscious intent to commit it). If Libet’s experiment suggests that consciousness does not initiate actions, the philosophical foundation of criminal law is called into question.

In practice, this issue already affects real debates. Defense attorneys argue that injuries to the prefrontal cortex (responsible for impulse inhibition, or Libet’s “veto”) reduce the capacity for self-control. Neuroscientists have been called as expert witnesses in court to present evidence that the ability to “veto” impulses can be impaired neurobiologically. If free will is the capacity to veto, losing that capacity changes culpability.

Attention: legal dilemma

If we accept that free will is only the power to veto, and that injuries or neurological conditions can eliminate that power, the legal system needs to rethink the very notion of guilt. We move from “you chose to do this” to “could your brain veto that impulse?”

Neurolaw: the emerging field

Neurolaw is the interdisciplinary field between neuroscience and law. It investigates questions such as: Can fMRI evidence be presented in trials? Can brain tumors mitigate responsibility? Does adolescent neuroscience justify different sentences? All these questions involve the Libet model.

Implications for mental health and education

Psychiatry and psychotherapy are based on the premise that patients have some degree of agency over their behavior. Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), for example, trains patients to recognize automatic thoughts and choose not to act on them. This is essentially training Libet’s veto: learning to identify unconscious impulses and block dysfunctional responses.

In education, the assumption of agency is equally central. Teaching means believing that the student can direct their attention, control impulses, and reorganize patterns of thought. Neuroplasticity confirms that the brain changes with training and practice, but the question of who directs this change remains. If Libet’s model is correct, education does not shape the will: it shapes the ability to veto impulses that arise without our permission.

Research: mindfulness and the veto

Neuroimaging studies show that mindfulness practice strengthens the medial prefrontal cortex and the connections between the prefrontal cortex and the amygdala—the very areas involved in inhibiting automatic responses. In Libetian terms, meditation is veto training: expanding the 200ms window between impulse and action.
