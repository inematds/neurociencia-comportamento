MODULE 1.2

Heuristics: the shortcuts that mislead us

Kahneman & Tversky · Availability, representativeness, anchoring

6 topics ~30 min Foundation
1

What heuristics are

Heuristics are simplified mental strategies the brain uses to make quick decisions under uncertainty. They are tools of System 1: rules of thumb that evolved over millions of years to help us survive in dangerous and unpredictable environments.

The key point is that heuristics are not errors. They are evolutionary adaptations that work well in most contexts. The problem arises when they are applied in contexts they weren't designed for: financial markets, complex medical decisions, evaluating statistics.

Kahneman vs. Gigerenzer

Gerd Gigerenzer argues that heuristics are “rational” when the environment is suitable: a firefighter who “senses” danger is using heuristics adaptively. Kahneman focuses on contexts where they fail. Both are right, in different domains.

Availability heuristic

We estimate the probability of something by how easily examples come to mind. If I can easily recall cases of shark attacks (because the media shows them in gruesome detail), I conclude they’re common. In reality, lightning kills far more people than sharks do.

Social media turned the availability heuristic into a tool for mass manipulation. Emotional and extreme content is amplified by the algorithm, becoming "more available" in users' memories. The result: distorted risk perception, disproportionate fear, and decisions based on virality, not reality.

When it works

  • ✓Assessing dangers in familiar environments
  • ✓Estimating the frequency of everyday events
  • ✓Quick decisions in an emergency

When it fails

  • ✗Assessing risks after exposure to biased media
  • ✗Judging the probability of rare events
  • ✗Estimating risk in statistical contexts

Social media as an amplifier

Engagement algorithms prioritize content that triggers fear, anger, and outrage. This content becomes more “available” in memory, distorting millions of people’s perception of risk at the same time. The result is artificial availability on an industrial scale.

Representativeness heuristic

We classify things by how closely they resemble a mental prototype, ignoring actual statistics. Kahneman’s classic example: “Steve is shy, organized, meticulous, and likes order.” Is Steve more likely to be a librarian or a farmer? Most people answer librarian, but there are many more farmers in the world.

The error is called "base-rate neglect": ignoring the base rate (the actual proportion in the population) in favor of resemblance to a stereotype. Representativeness is the cognitive basis of prejudice: we judge people by how much they appear to belong to a group, not by individual evidence.

The Linda Problem

"Linda is 31, single, outgoing, and has a degree in philosophy. In college, she was concerned about discrimination and social justice." Which is more likely: (A) Linda is a bank teller, or (B) Linda is a bank teller and a feminist activist? Most people choose B, but mathematically A is always more likely than A+B. This is the conjunction fallacy.

Research: stereotypes and medical diagnosis

Doctors who assess patients based on representativeness (the patient "looks like" they have disease X) make more mistakes than those who analyze epidemiological data. The representativeness heuristic is one documented cause of diagnostic error.

Anchoring heuristic

The first information we receive about a subject acts as an anchor that distorts all subsequent evaluations. In Kahneman and Tversky’s classic experiment, participants spun a roulette wheel (rigged to land on 10 or 65) and then estimated the percentage of African countries in the UN.

Those who got 10 on the roulette wheel estimated ~25 countries. Those who got 65 estimated ~45. A completely random and irrelevant number significantly changed their judgment. The anchor works even when we know it’s arbitrary, even when we try to correct for it consciously.

Everyday examples of anchoring

  • ✓A R$200 product seems cheap next to one priced at R$500
  • ✓The first offer in a negotiation sets the range
  • ✓Tip suggestion (15/18/20%) anchors choice
  • ✓Previous salary anchors a new job offer

Anchoring manipulation

  • ✗"Original price R$999" crossed out (artificial anchor)
  • ✗Absurd requests in negotiation (extreme anchor)
  • ✗Very high initial fines to "negotiate" a reduction
  • ✗Inflated metrics in presentations (vanity metrics)

Practical defense

In negotiations, whoever makes the first offer anchors the outcome. If someone anchors first with an outrageous value, the best defense is to explicitly reject the anchor (“that number doesn’t make sense, let’s start over”) and make your own offer.

Affect heuristic

Paul Slovic discovered that we judge risks and benefits based on feelings, not data. If something seems good to us (positive feeling), we automatically minimize the risks and maximize the benefits. If it seems bad to us (negative feeling), we do the opposite.

Nuclear energy is the classic case: despite being statistically one of the safest energy sources (deaths per kWh), it triggers visceral fear. Driving, on the other hand, kills hundreds of thousands each year, but seems “normal.” Emotion, not statistics, governs risk perception.

When heuristics work (and when they fail)

Heuristics are efficient in stable, familiar environments with rapid feedback. An experienced firefighter who “senses” that the floor is about to collapse is using patterns learned from hundreds of similar situations. In this context, the heuristic is faster and more reliable than any deliberate analysis.

Heuristics fail in complex, unfamiliar environments or when feedback is delayed. Financial markets, political decisions, and rare medical diagnoses are examples where relying on heuristics leads to systematic errors. Metacognition (knowing when you are using a heuristic) is the skill that separates good decisions from terrible ones.

High-validity environments

  • ✓Fast, clear feedback
  • ✓Regular and repetitive patterns
  • ✓Relevant accumulated experience
  • ✓E.g.: chess, firefighters, pilots

Low-validity environments

  • ✗Delayed or ambiguous feedback
  • ✗High complexity, many variables
  • ✗Rare or unprecedented situations
  • ✗E.g.: financial markets, politics, epidemics

Metacognition as a defense

The best protection against misapplied heuristics isn't to eliminate them (impossible), but to develop metacognition: the ability to notice, "I'm using a mental shortcut right now," and assess whether the context is appropriate for that shortcut.
