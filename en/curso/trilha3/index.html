3.1

Neuroplasticity: The Brain That Remolds Itself

The brain's ability to reorganize its structure and function in response to experience.

Until the mid-20th century, neuroscientists believed that the adult brain was a fixed, unchanging structure. Once formed, it would not create new connections or reorganize itself. This view is wrong. Today we know that the brain continuously remodels itself in response to experience, learning, and even injuries.

"Neurons that fire together wire together" (Donald Hebb, 1949). Repetition strengthens synapses (consolidation), and disuse weakens them (synaptic pruning). This is the basic cellular mechanism of learning.

Three main forms: (1) synaptic plasticity, which changes the strength of existing connections; (2) neurogenesis, the generation of new neurons in the hippocampus, essential for memory; (3) cortical reorganization, where brain regions take on new functions after injury or intensive use.

London taxi drivers have larger hippocampi from navigating without GPS. Musicians develop more robust motor and auditory regions. Meditators show measurable changes in attention and emotional regulation. They all demonstrate that intensive use physically reshapes the brain.

Deliberate practice (Ericsson) activates neuroplasticity in a targeted way. Sleep consolidates memories, transferring information from the hippocampus to the neocortex. Without adequate sleep, consolidation fails and learning suffers.

Chronic stress harms neuroplasticity, especially in the hippocampus, reducing neurogenesis and weakening connections. Elevated cortisol damages neurons. Although the adult brain is far more malleable than once thought, that malleability depends on favorable conditions.

3.2

Dopamine and the Reward System

Dopamine isn't "pleasure"—it's anticipation and motivation. The "wanting," not the "liking."

Dopamine is not the pleasure neurotransmitter. It is the neurotransmitter of anticipation and motivation. The crucial distinction: "wanting" (desiring, seeking) vs. "liking" (enjoying, feeling pleasure). Dopamine mediates wanting, not liking.

The reward system centers on the mesolimbic circuit: the Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA) produces dopamine and sends it to the Nucleus Accumbens (NAc). Dopamine is released in anticipation, especially when the reward is unpredictable.

Skinner (1950s) demonstrated that animals and humans respond more intensely to variable (unpredictable) rewards than to constant ones. This principle underlies slot machines, social media, and notifications. Variability keeps dopamine neurons hyperactivated.

Sean Parker (Facebook co-founder, 2017) admitted that social media design deliberately exploits “a vulnerability in human psychology.” Likes create dopamine loops that lead to behavioral dependence. You don’t know whether the next scroll will turn up something interesting—that uncertainty keeps your hand scrolling.

Dopaminergic circuits are central to reinforcement learning. The brain learns by predicting rewards and adjusting behavior based on the "prediction error." When the reward exceeds expectations, dopamine surges. When it falls short, dopamine decreases.

Chronic overactivation of dopaminergic circuits raises the response threshold. The brain needs more stimulation to feel the same effect. This is the mechanism behind tolerance in all addictions, whether chemical or behavioral.

3.3

Amygdala, Fear, and Emotional Decision-Making

The almond-shaped structure that operates faster than logic and hijacks your decisions.

The amygdala is an almond-shaped structure in the limbic system, central to processing fear and emotions. It operates faster than the prefrontal cortex (the logical/rational part of the brain), which means emotional responses can precede conscious thought.

Daniel Goleman coined the term "amygdala hijack": the amygdala "hijacks" higher cognitive functions when it detects a threat. Emotional responses happen before rational processing. That's why you react to a startle before understanding what happened.

The pain of losing R$100 is 1.5 to 2 times as intense as the pleasure of gaining R$100. The amygdala is directly involved: threats of loss activate the fear circuit more intensely than promises of gain activate the pleasure circuit.

Fear-based messages are more accessible and impactful than positive messages. Campaigns based on fear (immigrants, crime, economic collapse) access System 1 and bypass rational processing. The amygdala makes these messages visceral and memorable.

The prefrontal cortex can modulate the amygdala’s responses, acting as an emotional "brake." However, under stress, fatigue, or time pressure, this control fails and the amygdala takes over. That’s why decisions made under pressure tend to be more emotional than rational.

If the amygdala can hijack reason and emotions come before conscious thought, how much of our decision-making is actually “rational”? This question connects the neuroscience of behavior directly to the philosophy of mind and the free will debate in Track 5.
