MODULE 4.2

Nudge Theory: Choice Architecture

How small changes in the decision-making environment change behavior on a large scale without banning anything, changing incentives, or needing to convince anyone.

1

What a nudge is

In 2008, Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein published “Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth, and Happiness.” The central thesis: it is possible to change people’s behavior predictably without banning any options or significantly changing economic incentives.

A nudge is any aspect of the choice architecture that changes behavior in a predictable way. It is not an order. It is not a fine. It is not a subsidy. It is an adjustment to the environment in which the decision takes place. Changing the position of foods in the cafeteria so that fruit is at eye level is a nudge. Banning French fries is not.

✓ What a nudge is

  • •Change the default (opt-out instead of opt-in)
  • •Reduce friction (fewer clicks, fewer forms)
  • •Make information visible (salience)
  • •Use social norms (“9 out of 10 people do X”)

✕ What a nudge isn't

  • •Prohibit options (banning)
  • •Fines and financial penalties
  • •Subsidies that change economic incentives
  • •Compulsory legal mandates
Nudge Choice architecture Thaler & Sunstein Libertarian paternalism

Connection to 4.1: The nudge works only because Homo Economicus doesn't exist. If we were perfectly rational, the fruit's position on the shelf would be irrelevant. But we're human: we buy what's at eye level.

2

The power of the default

Of all nudges, the most powerful is also the simplest: the preselected option. When a form already has a box checked, most people simply don't uncheck it. Not because they agree. Because unchecking it takes effort, and the brain prefers the path of least resistance.

The most studied case is organ donation. Countries with a system opt-out (you are a donor by default and must actively opt out) have donation rates above 90%. Countries with a system opt-in (you must actively register) stay below 20%. The only difference is the default.

Same population, same European culture, same question. The only variable: the default.

✓ Helpful defaults

  • •Retirement plan with automatic enrollment
  • •Printer configured for double-sided printing
  • •Renewable energy as the default option
  • •Opt-out organ donation

✕ Defaults that exploit

  • •Subscriptions with hidden automatic renewal
  • •Preselected travel insurance when buying a ticket
  • •Data sharing enabled by default
  • •Premium plan as the default option
Default effect Opt-in vs. opt-out Status quo bias Decision inertia

Why it works: The default exploits three biases at once: status quo bias (we prefer not to change), loss aversion (changing could make things worse), and cognitive effort (making decisions is tiring). It’s a behavioral 3-in-1.

3

Simplification and salience

Each extra step between intention and action is a point where people may give up. Each extra field on a form means one fewer person completes the process. Simplification is a nudge because it removes friction: it makes the desired path easier to follow.

Salience works in the opposite way: instead of removing barriers, it highlights the desired option. The fruit at eye level in the cafeteria. The calorie notice on the menu. The nutrition traffic light on the package. When relevant information is visible at the moment of decision, the decision changes.

Examples of simplification that changed outcomes

US

Simplified FAFSA (U.S.)

University financial aid form reduced from 100+ to ~30 questions. Applications increased by 8 percentage points.

UK

Tax letter (UK BIT)

Simplified text + deadline highlighted in red. On-time payments rose 5%.

BR

Pix (Brazil, 2020)

Instant transfer in 2 taps versus TED/DOC with a bank, account, and check digit. Mass adoption in months.

Simplification Salience Friction Hassle factor

Golden rule: If you want people to do something, make it easy. If you want them to stop, make it hard. Every extra click is a filter. Every form is a barrier. Simplicity is power.

4

Feedback and social proof

Humans need immediate feedback to adjust their behavior. The problem: many consequences of our actions are invisible. You don't see the CO2 your car emits. You don't feel your cholesterol rising. You don't notice your bank account draining until the statement arrives. Feedback nudges make these consequences visible at the moment of action.

Social proof is equally powerful: when we know others are doing something, we feel pressure to do it too. The UK’s Behavioural Insights Team (BIT) tested this with taxes. A simple sentence added to a collection letter changed the behavior of millions of people.

UK BIT experiment: taxes and social proof

Letters about overdue taxes were sent with different messages. The version with local social proof had the best results:

A Standard letter (control)
baseline
B "Most people pay on time"
+5%
C "9 out of 10 people in your neighborhood pay on time"
+15%

The closer and more specific the social reference, the greater the effect. “Your neighborhood” is more powerful than “most people.”

✓ Effective feedback

  • •Speed display with an emoji (smile/frown)
  • •Energy bill with comparison to neighbors
  • •Spending app with real-time alerts
  • •Nutrition traffic light on packaging

✕ Feedback that fails

  • •Incomprehensible technical information
  • •Feedback delayed too long (monthly statement)
  • •Data without a reference point
  • •Excessive metrics (information paralysis)

5

Save More Tomorrow (SMarT)

Thaler and Shlomo Benartzi designed what may be the most elegant nudge in history: the Save More Tomorrow program. Instead of asking employees to save now (when the brain resists), the program only asked them to agree to allocate future salary increases for savings.

The psychological trick: no one loses money today. The raise you have not received yet does not “belong” to your present self, so loss aversion does not kick in. And because future raises are automatically redirected, inertia works in your favor instead of against you.

SMarT Results (Thaler & Benartzi, 2004)

YEAR 0

3.5% of the salary saved. Employees agree to allocate future raises.

YEAR 1

6.5% of the salary saved. First raise automatically redirected.

YEAR 2

9.4% of the salary saved. Second raise. No one felt a "loss".

YEAR 3

13.6% of the salary saved. Almost 4x the initial amount, with no pain.

SMarT explores three principles at once: precommitment (deciding today about the future is easier), inertia (once enrolled, nobody leaves) and loss aversion circumvented (you can't lose what you don't have yet). The program was so successful that it became public policy in the U.S. through the Pension Protection Act of 2006.

Save More Tomorrow Precommitment Inertia in favor Loss aversion

Why it's brilliant: SMarT does not require willpower, prohibit anything, change incentives, or cost the employer almost anything. It simply redesigns the moment and structure of the decision. This is choice architecture at its best.

6

Nudges in Brazil and Ethical Limits

Brazil was not left out of the nudge wave. In 2014, the city government of Rio de Janeiro created the NudgeRio, the first behavioral economics unit applied to municipal public policy in Latin America. In São Paulo, the Nudge//SP worked on health, education, and fundraising projects.

At NudgeRio, one of the best-known projects involved property tax collection: redesigning payment forms with simplified language, visually highlighting the deadline, and using social norms ("most residents in your neighborhood have already paid"). Result: a measurable increase in on-time payments.

Nudge Units Around the World

2010

UK BIT (Behavioural Insights Team). First government nudge unit. A model for the world.

2014

NudgeRio (Brazil). SBST (US, Obama). Nudge units spread around the globe.

2015

Nudge//SP. The World Bank and OECD adopt behavioral units.

2020+

More than 200 units of nudge in governments in 60+ countries. Nudge goes mainstream.

But when does a nudge stop being helpful and become manipulation? Thaler and Sunstein call their approach libertarian paternalism: paternalistic because it steers, libertarian because it doesn't prohibit. Critics ask: who decides what counts as "good" behavior? What if the government uses nudges to serve its own interests?

✓ Ethical nudge (transparent)

  • •Benefits those who are nudged (health, savings)
  • •The option to leave is easy and visible
  • •Based on scientific evidence
  • •Transparent and auditable design process

✕ Sludge (manipulation)

  • •Benefits those who nudge (companies, government)
  • •Making it hard or hidden to exit (dark patterns)
  • •Explores biases instead of working around them
  • •"Cancel subscription" in 12 steps
NudgeRio Libertarian paternalism Sludge Dark patterns

Thaler’s test: "A nudge is ethical if you'd feel comfortable explaining it openly to the person being nudged." If it has to be hidden to work, it's not a nudge. It's manipulation.

Module 4.2 Summary

  • ✓A nudge is any factor that predictably alters behavior without forbidding options or significantly changing incentives.
  • ✓The default is the most powerful nudge: opt-out countries have 85-99% organ donors vs. 4-27% in opt-in countries.
  • ✓Simplification removes friction (fewer steps = greater uptake). Salience highlights the desired option at the right moment.
  • ✓Immediate feedback and local social proof ("9 out of 10 people in your neighborhood") are high-impact behavioral levers.
  • ✓SMarT raised savings from 3.5% to 13.6% of salary in 3 years, without pain or prohibition.
  • ✓NudgeRio and Nudge//SP brought behavioral economics to Brazil. But a nudge becomes manipulation when it benefits the person doing the nudging, not the person being nudged.
