MODULE 4.1

The Critique of Homo Economicus

How Thaler and Ariely destroyed the myth of the perfectly rational agent and revealed the predictable irrationality that governs our economic decisions.

1

The Homo Economicus model

For more than a century, the dominant economic theory treated human beings as perfect calculating machines. Homo Economicus was the model: an agent with stable and transitive preferences who processes all available information, calculates the expected utility of each option, and chooses the one that maximizes their benefit.

This model was mathematically elegant. It allowed for equations, graphs, and predictions. It had one problem: it did not describe any real human being.

✓ What the model assumed

  • •Perfect rationality in every decision
  • •Preferences that don't change with context
  • •Full access to all relevant information
  • •Ability to process infinite options

✕ What reality shows

  • •Decisions influenced by emotions and context
  • •Preferences that change depending on how they're presented
  • •Limited and biased information
  • •Paralysis in the face of too many options
Perfect rationality Expected utility Transitive preferences Maximization

Connection to T1: Homo Economicus is essentially a "pure System 2 being" (Kahneman). It never uses shortcuts, never makes mistakes, and never gets tired. This being does not exist.

2

The endowment effect

Richard Thaler discovered something classical economics said was impossible: simply owning something changes the value we assign to it. A mug you received just 5 minutes ago already "feels" more valuable to you than to someone who doesn't have one.

In the classic experiment, participants given mugs asked for an average of $7.12 to sell them (WTA, willingness to accept). Those without a mug offered an average of $2.87 to buy one (WTP, willingness to pay). The same object, twice the value, depending only on who "owns" it.

Mug experiment (Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, 1990)

$7.12

WTA (price to sell)

who already has the mug

$2.87

WTP (price to buy)

who does not have the mug

Why does this matter? Because according to classical economics, WTA and WTP should be equal. Whether you own something or not shouldn't change its objective value. But the brain doesn't work with objective values: it works with losses and gains relative to a reference point.

Endowment Effect Loss aversion Status quo bias WTA vs WTP

Practical implication: Investors hold on to losing stocks because of the endowment effect. "It's mine now; I can't sell it for less than I paid." The loss is cognitive, not financial.

3

Framing and framing

If humans were rational, the way an option is presented should not affect the decision. But it does. Deeply. Thaler and Kahneman demonstrated that framing changes choices even when the options are logically identical.

"90% survival rate" and "10% mortality rate" are mathematically identical. But patients, doctors, and jurors react radically differently to each framing. Whoever controls the frame controls the decision.

✓ Framing works in

  • •Medical decisions (surgery vs. medication)
  • •Prices (discount vs. surcharge)
  • •Public policy (employment vs. unemployment)
  • •Negotiations (mutual gain vs. concession)

✕ What Homo Economicus would say

  • •"Irrelevant, they're the same data"
  • •"The form shouldn't affect the content"
  • •"Preferences don't change with words"
  • •"This violates descriptive invariance"
Framing effect Prospect Theory Gains vs. losses Descriptive invariance
4

Limited self-control and temporal discounting

Thaler observed that people consistently promise themselves they’ll do in the future what they can’t manage in the present. You commit to starting the diet on Monday, saving next month, studying during vacation. Your future self is heroic. Your present self wants ice cream.

The temporal battle of self-control

JAN

Promise (future self)

"Starting in February, I'll save 20% of my salary."

FEB

Reality (present self)

"There's Carnival this month; I'll start in March."

TEN

Result

Savings: R$0. Promise renewed for next year.

This happens because of the hyperbolic discounting: the brain discounts the value of future rewards disproportionately. R$100 now is worth more than R$110 tomorrow, but R$100 in 30 days and R$110 in 31 days seem equivalent. The time gap distorts our perception of value.

Thaler called this "planner vs. doer": the planner (prefrontal) sets goals; the doer (limbic) operates in the present. The doer almost always wins.

Hyperbolic discounting Present bias Planner vs. doer Precommitment
5

Predictably Irrational: Dan Ariely

In "Predictably Irrational" (2008), Dan Ariely took the critique a step further: human errors are not only real, they are systematically predictable. They are not random noise. They are patterns that can be mapped, studied, and exploited (or mitigated).

The Economist experiment (Ariely, 2008)

Ariely asked MIT students to choose among subscription options for The Economist magazine:

A Digital
R$59
B Printed
R$125
C Digital + Print
R$125

Option B (the print edition at R$125) seems absurd: why would anyone pay the same price for only the print edition? But that's exactly the point. Option B serves as decoy (decoy): makes option C seem irresistible. Without option B, most people chose A. With B present, most people switched to C.

Ariely's lesson: We don't decide in absolute terms. We decide by comparison. Whoever adds an inferior comparator manipulates the choice.

Predictable irrationality Decoy Effect Price anchoring Decision by comparison
6

Richard Thaler: 2017 Nobel Prize and Legacy

In 2017, Richard Thaler received the Nobel Prize in Economics “for his contributions to behavioral economics.” It was formal recognition that economics needed real humans, not rational robots, in its models.

The story of the fall of Homo Economicus

1979

Kahneman & Tversky: Prospect Theory. Humans weigh losses ~2x more than gains.

1980

Thaler: publishes "Toward a Positive Theory of Consumer Choice." Academic heresy at the time.

2002

Kahneman receives the Nobel Prize in Economics (as a psychologist). First sign of change.

2008

Thaler & Sunstein: "Nudge". Ariely: "Predictably Irrational." Behavioral economics reaches the general public.

2010

UK BIT: first government Nudge Unit. Public policy based on behavioral evidence.

2017

Thaler: Nobel Prize in Economics. "Econs vs Humans" becomes a paradigm.

Thaler distinguished two types of agents in his models: Econs (the rational agents of classical theory) and Humans (how people actually behave). His book "Misbehaving" (2015) documents how the economics profession resisted for decades admitting that Humans don't behave like Econs.

Module 4.1 Summary

  • ✓Homo Economicus never existed. Humans are not rational, do not have stable preferences, and do not maximize utility.
  • ✓Endowment effect: owning something changes its perceived value. Loss aversion outweighs the attraction of gains.
  • ✓Framing: the same information, presented in different ways, leads to opposite decisions.
  • ✓Self-control: the present self beats the future self. Hyperbolic discounting distorts temporal value.
  • ✓Ariely: irrationality is predictable. Decoys and comparisons systematically manipulate choices.
  • ✓Thaler (2017 Nobel Prize): established behavioral economics as a central field in the discipline.
