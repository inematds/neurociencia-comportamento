4.1

The Critique of Homo Economicus

Thaler (2017), Ariely (2008) · 6 topics

What is

Model of classical economics: a perfectly rational agent, with stable preferences, who maximizes utility with complete information. The foundation of neoclassical microeconomics for more than a century.

Why learn

To understand why traditional economic policies fail: they assume a human being who does not exist.

Key concepts

Perfect rationality, expected utility, transitive preferences, complete information.

What is

Thaler showed that people value what they already own more than what they could acquire. A mug you’ve received as a gift is "worth" more than the same mug on the shelf.

Why learn

Explains why people hold on to losing stocks, resist changes in plans, and overvalue their own possessions.

Key concepts

Endowment effect, loss aversion, status quo bias, WTA vs WTP.

What is

The way an option is presented drastically changes the choice. "90% survival" vs. "10% mortality" are identical, but lead to opposite decisions.

Why learn

Framing is the basis of manipulation in marketing, politics, and negotiation. Whoever frames the issue controls it.

Key concepts

Framing effect, gains vs. losses, positive/negative framing, Prospect Theory.

What is

Humans promise their future selves what they don't follow through on in the present. Temporal distance makes commitments seem easy. When the time comes, the present self wins over the future self.

Why learn

Explains procrastination, debt, diet failures, and why private retirement plans need nudges.

Key concepts

Hyperbolic discounting, present bias, planner vs. doer, precommitment.

What is

Ariely (2008) showed that human errors aren’t random: they’re systematically predictable. They can be mapped, studied, and exploited or mitigated. The Economist subscription options experiment proves this.

Why learn

If irrationality is predictable, it can be used both to manipulate and to protect.

Key concepts

Predictable irrationality, the decoy effect, price anchoring, and free as a trigger.

What is

Richard Thaler received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2017 for incorporating psychologically realistic assumptions into economic theory. His work connects Kahneman, nudges, and public policy.

Why learn

Provide context on how behavioral economics went from academic heresy to a tool of government in less than 20 years.

Key concepts

Behavioral economics, libertarian paternalism, Misbehaving, Econs vs Humans.

4.2

Nudge Theory: Choice Architecture

Thaler & Sunstein (2008) · 6 topics

What is

A factor that predictably changes behavior without banning options or significantly changing economic incentives. The decision context profoundly affects the decision.

Why learn

Governments, companies, and digital platforms use nudges. Recognizing them is the first step toward avoiding being guided without your knowledge.

Key concepts

Nudge, choice architecture, choice architect, libertarian paternalism.

What is

The preselected option changes behavior on a mass scale. Organ donation with opt-out vs. opt-in: countries where donation is preselected have 85-99% donors vs. 4-27%.

Why learn

Whoever sets the default controls the outcome. This applies to public policy, software, and digital services.

Key concepts

Default effect, opt-in vs. opt-out, status quo bias, decision inertia.

What is

Three complementary principles: fewer steps = greater uptake (simplification); make the desired option more visible (salience); immediate, tangible consequences (feedback).

Why learn

Together, these three principles explain why long forms fail, why simple menus sell more, and why consumption meters work.

Key concepts

Friction, sludge (anti-nudge), attention economics, feedback loops.

What is

Telling people what most others do influences behavior. UK BIT: "9 out of 10 people in your neighborhood pay their taxes on time" increased tax payments by 15%.

Why learn

Social proof is the most widely replicated nudge in the world and connects behavioral economics with social psychology (Track 2).

Key concepts

Social proof, descriptive vs. injunctive norms, Behavioural Insights Team, EAST framework.

What is

SMarT (Thaler & Benartzi): commit future salary increases to savings. Participation rose from 3.5% to 13.6% in 3 years. Brazil: NudgeRio (IPTU), Nudge//SP.

Why learn

Demonstrates that nudges work at scale, at near-zero cost and with measurable impact.

Key concepts

SMarT, temporal precommitment, Nudge Units, experimental evidence in public policy.

What is

Criticism: disguised manipulation (sludge), limited effectiveness for complex behaviors, ethical questions about who decides what is “best” for whom. Dark nudges in digital design.

Why learn

A nudge is not a panacea. Understanding its limits helps you avoid overestimating it and makes room for complementary solutions.

Key concepts

Sludge, dark nudges, boosts (an alternative), autonomy vs. efficiency, design ethics.

4.3

Mental Accounting and the Paradox of Choice

Thaler, Schwartz · 6 topics

What is

We treat money differently depending on its source and destination. R$500 from betting isn't treated like R$500 in salary, even though they're economically identical.

Why learn

Explains why people spend bonuses in ways they would never spend their salaries, and why “mental accounts” lead to poor financial decisions.

Key concepts

Mental accounting, fungibility, separate mental accounts, house money effect.

What is

We create mental “drawers” for different spending categories. “Leisure” money doesn’t mix with “bills.” This violates the economic principle of fungibility and leads to suboptimization.

Why learn

Understanding your mental compartments is the first step toward making more rational financial decisions.

Key concepts

Hedonic editing, sunk cost fallacy, segregation of gains, integration of losses.

What is

Schwartz (2004): more options do not lead to better decisions. The jam study (Iyengar & Lepper): the stand with 24 flavors attracted more visitors but sold 10x less than the stand with 6.

Why learn

In an era of endless options, decision paralysis is an epidemic. Understanding the paradox is essential for design, business, and life.

Key concepts

Choice overload, the paradox of choice, choice paralysis, satisficing vs. maximizing.

What is

More options increase post-decision regret and the perceived opportunity cost. When you choose 1 out of 30, you "lost" 29 alternatives. When you choose 1 out of 3, you lost only 2.

Why learn

Anticipated regret paralyzes decisions, while retrospective regret erodes satisfaction.

Key concepts

Regret aversion, FOMO, counterfactual thinking, psychological opportunity cost.

What is

Schwartz distinguishes two profiles: maximizers (who seek the best possible option and end up dissatisfied) and satisficers (who seek “good enough” and feel satisfied). Maximizers earn more but are less happy.

Why learn

Identifying your profile and adopting satisficing strategies in non-critical areas improves well-being.

Key concepts

Satisficing (Simon), maximizing, good enough, social comparison.

What is

Platforms with huge catalogs invest billions in recommendation algorithms to reduce choice paralysis. Netflix, Spotify, and Amazon are examples of anti-paradox architecture.

Why learn

Connects behavioral economics with digital design and the algorithmic biases of Track 6.

Key concepts

Algorithmic curation, recommendation engines, filter bubbles, reduction of choice set.
