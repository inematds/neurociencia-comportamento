MODULE 4.3

Mental Accounting and the Paradox of Choice

Why we treat money differently depending on where it comes from, why we value what we already own more, and why more options can lead to worse decisions.

1

Mental accounting (Thaler)

Richard Thaler coined the term mental accounting (mental accounting) to describe how the brain organizes money into separate "accounts," even when, objectively, money is fungible: R$1 is R$1, no matter where it came from.

But that’s not how we think. R$500 won in a bet is treated as "house money" and spent more easily. R$500 from a salary is "hard-earned money" and protected more carefully. The same amount, opposite behaviors. The mental source of the money changes how we use it.

✓ Common mental accounts

  • •"Vacation money" (untouchable for bills)
  • •"Gift money" (spent on frivolities)
  • •"Emergency savings" (sacred)
  • •"13th-month salary" (as a bonus, not as income)

✕ What rationality would say

  • •Money is fungible: R$1 = R$1
  • •Origin does not affect objective value
  • •All expenses must compete equally
  • •Separating accounts is an accounting convention
Mental accounting Fungibility House money effect Mental accounts

Thaler’s classic example: You bought a theater ticket for R$200. When you arrive, you discover that you lost the ticket. Do you buy another one? Most people say “no.” Now: you were going to buy a ticket at the box office. When you arrive, you discover that you lost R$200 in cash. Do you still buy the ticket? Most people say “yes.” The loss is identical. The mental accounting is different.

2

Endowment Effect (endowment effect)

The endowment effect, identified by Thaler and studied extensively by Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler, shows that people assign greater value to objects simply because they own them. It’s not accumulated emotional attachment. It happens instantly: you received the object 5 minutes ago and already demand more to sell it than you’d pay to buy it.

The Cup Experiment (Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler, 1990)

Participants in an experiment were divided into two groups: one received coffee cups from a university, the other received nothing. Then, they were invited to negotiate.

The same object. Whoever "owns" it assigns it 2.5x more value. Ownership distorts the perception of value.

The endowment effect connects directly to loss aversion by Kahneman and Tversky: losing something that is already "mine" hurts ~2x more than the pleasure of gaining something equivalent. Selling means losing. The brain resists.

3

Framing

Mental accounting has a close cousin: framing. While mental accounting separates money into fictional accounts, framing shows that way of presenting one piece of information radically changes the decision, even when the content is identical.

Tversky and Kahneman demonstrated this in the famous "Asian disease problem": when the option is presented as "saving 200 of 600 people," most people choose certainty. When it is presented as "400 people will die," most people prefer to take a risk. Mathematically, they are the same thing.

✓ Positive framing works for

  • •Convince patients (survival, cure)
  • •Marketing (economy, bonus, free)
  • •Public policy (jobs created)
  • •Negotiation (mutual gain)

✕ Negative framing works for

  • •Deter (risk of death, fine)
  • •Anti-smoking campaigns (what you stand to lose)
  • •Safety alerts (consequences)
  • •Urgency (last chance, you’ll miss out)
Framing effect Prospect Theory Gains vs. losses Descriptive invariance
4

Paradox of Choice (Schwartz)

Barry Schwartz, in "The Paradox of Choice" (2004), documented something counterintuitive: more options do not always lead to better decisions. In many cases, too many options lead to decision paralysis (choice overload), worse choices, and lower satisfaction with the outcome.

The study that opened up the field was Iyengar and Lepper’s jelly experiment (2000). At a supermarket stand, researchers alternated between 6 and 24 flavors of jelly. The result completely overturned classical economic intuition.

More variety attracted more people, but sold 10x less. Too many options paralyzed the purchase decision.

Schwartz distinguished two types of decision-maker: maximizers (seek the best possible option) and satisficers (accept the first "good enough" option). Maximizers suffer more from the paradox: the more options there are, the more they compare, the longer they take, and the less satisfied they feel.

Choice overload Paradox of choice Maximizer vs. Satisficer Decision paralysis

Practical implication: If you manage a product, restaurant, or store: reducing the menu can increase sales. Netflix discovered this: the endless catalog without recommendations was killing engagement. Curation matters as much as content.

5

Choice paralysis in the digital world

The digital world has amplified the paradox of choice to unprecedented levels. Netflix has more than 15.000 titles. Spotify offers more than 100 million songs. Amazon lists hundreds of options for any product. Without some filtering mechanism, making a decision becomes impossible. The user doesn't choose: they give up or scroll endlessly without watching anything.

The industry’s response was to invest billions in recommendation algorithms. Netflix estimates that 80% of the content people watch comes from algorithmic recommendations, not active searches. In practice, the algorithm is a computational nudge: it reduces the visible options to a manageable subset, eliminating paralysis. Spotify, YouTube, and TikTok do the same: automated curation as an antidote to the paradox.

Algorithmic curation as a response to the paradox

NETFL

80% of the content watched comes from recommendations. Each user sees a different "Netflix."

SPOT

Discover Weekly and personalized playlists reduce 100M+ songs to 30 a week.

TIKTK

For You Page eliminates choice entirely. The algorithm decides what you see.

AMZN

"Customers who bought this also bought" reduces a catalog of millions to 5–10 suggestions.

Too many options also create post-decision regret. With 3 options, you have 2 unchosen alternatives. With 30, you have 29 ghosts: "what if I had chosen that other one?" Schwartz showed that maximizers (people who seek the best possible option) suffer more: more freedom of choice can mean less happiness.

✓ Benefits of curation

  • •Reduces decision paralysis
  • •Increases satisfaction with the choice
  • •Allows discovery of relevant content
  • •Saves time and cognitive energy

✕ Risks of curation

  • •Filter bubble (echo chamber)
  • •Attention manipulation (engagement farming)
  • •Reduction in real freedom of choice
  • •Algorithm optimizes for the platform, not for you
Recommendation algorithm Filter bubble Automatic curation Computational Nudge

Irony: We resolve the paradox of choice by outsourcing the choice to machines. The modern consumer’s freedom of choice is largely the freedom to accept or reject suggestions from an algorithm that knows your patterns better than you do.

6

How to use this to your advantage

Understanding mental accounting, the endowment effect, framing, and the paradox of choice is more than theory. These concepts are practical tools for making better financial, professional, and personal decisions. Awareness of biases does not eliminate them, but it creates a layer of protection: a “meta-reasoning” that lets you question your first impression.

Thaler argues that fungibility is the golden rule: treat all money as money, regardless of its source. Schwartz recommends adopting a "satisficer" mindset for most decisions: set minimum criteria and accept the first option that meets them. Save maximizing for truly important decisions.

Practical strategies based on research

CM

Against mental accounting

Ask: "would I do the same thing if this money came from my salary?" If the answer is no, you're in a mental accounting trap.

ED

Against the endowment effect

Thaler’s technique: "If I didn’t have this, how much would I pay to get it?" If that’s less than the amount you’re asking to sell it for, the endowment effect is inflating its value.

FR

Against framing

Reframe the same situation in two opposite ways. If your preference changes, you’re reacting to the frame, not the content.

FOOT

Against the paradox of choice

Deliberately limit your options. Define criteria before researching. Adopt the "3 options maximum" rule for everyday decisions.

✓ When to maximize

  • •Career and profession decisions
  • •Buying a property or car
  • •Long-term investments
  • •Few high-impact decisions in life

✓ When to satisfice

  • •Restaurant for lunch
  • •Movie to watch tonight
  • •Supermarket shelf product
  • •Most everyday decisions
Fungibility Satisficing Meta-reasoning Debiasing

Thaler’s final message: "If you want people to make good decisions, make the decision easier." This applies both to those who design systems and those who are deciding: simplify, limit options, question frames, and treat money as money.

Module 4.3 Summary

  • ✓Mental accounting: we treat money differently depending on where it comes from. R$500 from a bet is not the same as R$500 in salary, even though they have the same value.
  • ✓Endowment effect: owning something instantly increases its perceived value. Sellers ask ~2.5x more than buyers offer for the same item.
  • ✓Framing: “90% survival” and “10% mortality” are identical, but lead to opposite decisions. Whoever controls the frame controls the decision.
  • ✓Paradox of choice: 6 jams sell 10x more than 24. More options attract but paralyze.
  • ✓Digital platforms solve the paradox with recommendation algorithms, but create bubbles and reduce real autonomy.
  • ✓Practical defenses: question the mental accounting, reframe, limit options, be a satisficer in everyday life.
