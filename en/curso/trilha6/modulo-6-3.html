"We Will Be Data": Bruzzo's thesis

In "Seremos Dados," Marcus Bruzzo proposes a fundamental reversal of the debate about artificial intelligence. The question that dominates popular culture and much of academia ("will AI be more intelligent than us?", "is AI conscious?", "will AI replace us?") is, for Bruzzo, the wrong question. It assumes the problem is one of comparison: human versus machine. But the real question is not about competition. It is about space.

What happens to human space as we progressively delegate our cognitive, creative, and decision-making functions to automated systems? The question is not whether AI writes better than a human, but what happens to the human capacity to write when billions of people stop exercising it. It is not about whether AI makes better decisions, but what happens to the human capacity to decide when decisions are outsourced. Bruzzo’s thesis is that the risk is not the machine’s superiority, but human atrophy.

The debate's reversal

The central question is not “Is AI superior or inferior to humans?” That is a comparative trap. The question is: what happens to human space when we delegate functions that make us human? Bruzzo’s answer: it shrinks.

The wrong question

  • ✗Is AI more intelligent than we are?
  • ✗Will AI replace us?
  • ✗Is AI conscious?
  • ✗Humans vs. machines (competitive frame)

Bruzzo's question

  • ●What happens to human space?
  • ●Which abilities atrophy through disuse?
  • ●Who controls the delegation systems?
  • ●What cannot be reduced to data?

2

Erosion of cognitive agency

The first dimension of risk identified by Bruzzo is the erosion of cognitive agency. We outsource memory to smartphones, spatial navigation to GPS, writing to generative AI, and calculation to calculators. Each delegation seems reasonable on its own: why memorize a phone number if the device stores it? Why learn to find your way around the city if GPS shows you the route? But the cumulative effect is a progressive atrophy of the capacities that make us cognitive agents.

The neuroscience behind this concern is neuroplasticity, studied in Track 3 of this course. The brain strengthens the circuits it uses and weakens the ones it doesn't. If we stop exercising spatial memory (because GPS takes care of it), the hippocampus loses measurable volume. If we stop practicing argumentative writing (because AI generates texts), the circuits involved in organizing thought weaken. This is not a metaphor: it is synaptic reorganization.

Cascade of cognitive delegation

Memory: Contacts, birthdays, appointments delegated to the smartphone. The hippocampus is used less.

Navigation: GPS replaces spatial navigation. Studies show reduced hippocampal volume in drivers who use GPS for years.

Writing: Generative AI drafts texts. The organization of thought through writing falls into disuse.

Decision: Algorithms recommend what to eat, watch, buy, and who to vote for. Deliberative capacity atrophies.

Neuroplasticity as a foundation

Unexercised abilities atrophy. This isn't cultural pessimism; it's neuroscience. The same plasticity that allows us to learn also allows us to unlearn. When we systematically delegate, the brain reorganizes for a world where these functions aren't needed.

3

Erosion of cultural diversity

The second dimension of risk is the erosion of cultural diversity. When billions of people use the same systems to generate text, images, music, and code, cultural production tends toward homogenization. Each large language model (LLM) was trained on a particular corpus, with particular biases, and optimized for particular statistical patterns. When that same model generates content for billions, it produces variations within a limited aesthetic space.

Bruzzo calls this the opposite of the "imaginary surplus." Human culture has historically produced more forms, styles, narratives, and modes of expression than any individual could consume. This abundance was the substrate of innovation: the unexpected, the strange, what made no immediate sense. When generation passes through an algorithmic funnel optimized for engagement and recognizable patterns, the tendency is to compress this diversity. Not eliminate it all at once, but gradually reduce it, like erosion.

Mechanisms of homogenization

  • ✗The same model generates for billions: variations within a restricted aesthetic space
  • ✗Engagement optimization favors the familiar over the unfamiliar
  • ✗Feedback loop: generated content trains future models
  • ✗Local traditions lose ground to an algorithmic “global style”

Imaginary surplus (what is lost)

  • ●Aesthetic forms that don't optimize any metric
  • ●Local narratives with no appeal to global scale
  • ●The strange, the unexpected, what doesn’t make immediate sense
  • ●Diversity as the foundation of cultural innovation

Connection to Track 6.2 (Epistemic Bubbles)

The epistemic bubbles studied in the previous module fragment our perception of reality. Here, the mechanism is complementary: it’s not that each group sees a different reality, but that everyone starts producing culture from the same mold. Fragmentation in perception, homogenization in production.

4

Transfer of power and opacity

The third dimension is the most directly political: the transfer of decision-making power to opaque systems. Decisions that affect real lives (granting credit, diagnosing health conditions, judicial sentencing, job selection, access to social benefits) are delegated to algorithms whose inner workings are not understandable even to their creators, much less to the people affected.

This opacity drastically reduces the possibility of challenging a decision. When a human denies a loan, the applicant can ask why and, in principle, argue against the decision. When an algorithm denies one, the answer is "the model said no." The technical explanation (a combination of weights across millions of parameters) cannot be translated into reasons that can be challenged. The result is an erosion of accountability: no one decides, so no one answers for it. The system makes the decision, but the system is not a moral agent.

The accountability problem

When an algorithm denies credit, employment, or parole, who is responsible? The developer who trained the model? The company that licensed it? The judge who followed its recommendation? The diffusion of responsibility makes challenging the decision almost impossible. A decision with no one accountable is power without restraint.

Domains of delegated decision-making

  • ✗Credit: algorithmic score determines access to financing
  • ✗Healthcare: automated triage prioritizes patients
  • ✗Justice: risk scores influence sentencing and bail
  • ✗Employment: AI filters résumés before any human review

Why opacity is dangerous

  • ✗Millions of parameters: inexplicable in human language
  • ✗No contestable reasons: “the model said no”
  • ✗Diffusion of responsibility: nobody “decided”
  • ✗Power without accountability: the most dangerous form of power

5

The useless as resistance

If Bruzzo’s diagnosis is bleak, his proposal for resistance is surprising: what remains human is the useless. Not useless as failure, but useless as a philosophical category. Art that optimizes no metric. Love that maximizes no utility. Play with no productive purpose. Conversations that exist simply for their own sake. A walk with no destination. Contemplation with no output. Silence that does not need to be filled.

This proposal engages with a long philosophical tradition. Aristotle considered contemplation (theoria) the highest activity precisely because it was the least useful in a practical sense. Kant saw aesthetic experience as "purposiveness without purpose." Hannah Arendt distinguished labor (biological necessity), work (the fabrication of objects), and action (free activity among equals), with action being the properly human dimension precisely because it has no instrumental utility. Bruzzo updates this tradition for the algorithmic age: resisting the technological colonization of the imagination means radically valuing what cannot be reduced to data.

The "useless" that makes us human

  • ●Art that doesn’t optimize engagement or sales
  • ●Love that doesn’t maximize utility or convenience
  • ●A game without productivity goals
  • ●Conversation that exists only for its own sake

Philosophical tradition of the useless

  • ●Aristotle: theoria (contemplation) as the highest activity
  • ●Kant: aesthetic experience as "purposiveness without purpose"
  • ●Arendt: free action among equals, without instrumental utility
  • ●Bruzzo: what cannot be reduced to data

Resistance, not Luddism

Bruzzo does not propose rejecting technology (Luddism). He proposes actively protecting and cultivating the spaces of human experience that cannot and should not be optimized. He is not against AI: he is for what AI cannot be. It is an ethical position, not a technophobic one.

Synthesis: what it means to be human in the age of AI

This module wraps up the course "Mente, Poder e Comportamento". We traced an arc that began with the mechanics of individual decision-making (Systems 1 and 2, heuristics, biases), moved through social influence (conformity, obedience, manipulation), explored the neural infrastructure (neuroplasticity, dopamine, amygdala), moved up to the economic systems that exploit these vulnerabilities (behavioral economics, nudge, mental accounting), questioned the philosophical foundations (free will, consciousness, semiotics), and reached the current frontier: what happens when machines inherit, amplify, and automate all of this.

Bruzzo’s answer, and this course’s final argument, is not a technical one. It is an ethical and existential answer. Being human in the age of AI does not mean competing with machines in efficiency, speed, or scale. It was never about that. It means insisting on the dimensions of experience that cannot be reduced to data, optimized by algorithms, or delegated to systems. Vulnerability. Ambiguity. Productive error. Useless beauty. Presence without purpose. Encountering another person who cannot be predicted, modeled, or replicated.

The course’s full arc

  • T1How we decide (biases, heuristics, System 1/2)
  • T2How we are influenced (conformity, obedience)
  • T3The neural infrastructure behind it all
  • T4How systems exploit our vulnerabilities
  • T5Philosophical foundations: mind, consciousness, meaning
  • T6The frontier: when machines inherit and amplify all of this

What remains irreducible

  • ●Vulnerability: making mistakes, failing, and growing from it
  • ●Ambiguity: dwelling on unanswered questions
  • ●Presence: being with the other person, without algorithmic mediation
  • ●Useless beauty: what exists without needing justification
  • ●Encounter: the unpredictable that no model can replicate

Final reflection

The question this course leaves you with isn't "will AI replace us?" That's the question driven by fear. The question that matters is: what, of everything we've learned about the mind, power, and behavior, will we protect? What will we practice, cultivate, defend, even when it's slower, less efficient, harder? What will we insist on doing with our own hands, our own voice, our own thinking—not because the machine can't, but because doing it is what makes us who we are?
